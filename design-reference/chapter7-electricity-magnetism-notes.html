Electricity

What energy actually means

Energy is simply the ability to do work — measured in joules (J). It never gets created or destroyed, only changes form. Tap each form to see where you'd find it.

The static shock you feel on a doorknob

Rub two different materials together and electrons transfer between them — the object that gains electrons becomes negatively charged, the one that loses them becomes positively charged. That imbalance is an electrostatic charge, and it's exactly what causes the shock when you touch metal after walking on carpet.

The rule that governs it allLike charges repel each other. Opposite charges attract. That single rule explains everything from why a charged comb picks up paper, to how an electroscope's gold leaf diverges to reveal a charge, to how lightning forms between charged clouds and the ground.

Naming the electrical quantities

Three quantities describe any circuit, each with its own unit and measuring tool:

🔋 Voltage

7.2

Series and Parallel Circuits

One path, or many?

A series circuit connects components one after another in a single loop — break it anywhere and everything stops. A parallel circuit splits into multiple separate paths — each branch keeps working even if another fails. That's exactly why your home's wiring is parallel: one blown bulb shouldn't kill power to the whole house.

Series
Parallel
Current: same everywhere (I = I₁ = I₂)  |  Voltage: splits across components (V = V₁ + V₂)  |  Resistance: adds up (R = R₁ + R₂)

Upside: simple, one switch controls everything. Downside: one broken component kills the whole circuit.
Current: splits across branches (I = I₁ + I₂)  |  Voltage: same across every branch (V = V₁ = V₂)  |  Resistance: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂

Upside: each appliance works independently. Downside: can't adjust voltage per appliance individually.

7.3

Magnetism

What makes something a magnet

Every magnet shares the same four properties. Tap each one.

Field lines never lie

The space around a magnet where its force acts is called the magnetic field, mapped out by field lines. These lines always run from north to south pole, get closer together where the field is stronger, and never cross one another.

Turning electricity into magnetism

An electromagnet is a temporary magnet — it only works while current flows through it. The right-hand grip rule tells you the direction of the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying wire: curl your right hand's fingers in the direction of the field, and your thumb points the direction of conventional current.
